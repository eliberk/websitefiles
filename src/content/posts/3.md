Before committing capital to Venezuelan oil and gas, investors need to understand how the legal framework for exploration and production actually works. Venezuela's oil sector operates under a specific contract model that governs the relationship between international partners and the state — and understanding it is the foundation of any credible investment thesis.

## The Role of PDVSA and the Venezuelan State

Venezuela's constitution establishes that all hydrocarbon resources belong to the state. Petróleos de Venezuela, S.A. (PDVSA) — the national oil company — acts as the primary vehicle through which the state manages its oil and gas assets.

This means that any international company or investment vehicle seeking to conduct E&P activity in Venezuela must do so through a formal contractual relationship with either PDVSA or the Venezuelan Ministry of Petroleum.

## The Joint Venture Model

The dominant legal structure for international E&P participation in Venezuela is the **mixed company (empresa mixta)** model, established under the 2001 Hydrocarbons Law and its subsequent reforms.

Under this model:

- PDVSA or a PDVSA subsidiary holds a **minimum 60% ownership stake** in the joint venture
- The international partner holds the remaining **up to 40%**
- The JV is granted operating rights over a specific block or area
- Production, costs, and revenues are shared according to the ownership structure and contract terms

This model has been used successfully by Chevron (which has maintained continuous operations in Venezuela through multiple political cycles), Repsol, ENI, and others.

## What "Contractually Secured" Means in Practice

When DerivaConnect describes its revenue as "contractually secured," this refers to several specific legal protections:

**1. Defined production entitlements**
The contract specifies exactly what percentage of production the partner is entitled to receive, calculated after cost recovery and royalty payments to the Venezuelan state.

**2. Cost recovery provisions**
Before profit splits are applied, the international partner is entitled to recover its operating and capital costs from production revenues — a standard feature of production sharing frameworks globally.

**3. Dispute resolution mechanisms**
Modern Venezuelan E&P contracts include international arbitration clauses (typically under ICSID or ICC rules), providing a neutral forum for resolving disputes outside Venezuelan courts.

**4. Sanctity of contract protections**
Venezuela has, through multiple administrations, demonstrated a strategic interest in maintaining relationships with international operators who are actively producing — because production is the source of state revenue.

## Why Contract Risk Is Manageable

Venezuela has nationalized foreign oil assets before — most notably in 2007, when it renegotiated terms with several majors operating in the Orinoco Belt. However, several factors distinguish today's environment:

- Companies that accepted the new JV terms (like Chevron and ENI) continued operating without interruption
- Venezuela's fiscal dependency on oil revenues gives it a strong incentive to keep producing partners operational
- The current government has actively courted new international partnerships to reverse production declines
- The revised contract frameworks include stronger protections than previous agreements

None of this eliminates risk. Venezuelan oil investment carries real political and operational risk that investors must understand. But characterizing Venezuela as an uncontractable environment misreads both the history and the current incentive structure.

## The Due Diligence Checklist

For any Venezuela E&P investment, investors should verify:

- The specific legal entity holding the production rights
- The block location, reserve estimates, and production history
- The ownership and cost recovery structure
- Dispute resolution provisions
- The operating partner's track record in Venezuela
